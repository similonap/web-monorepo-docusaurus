1. AI makes software cheaper, and cheap things get used more.
When steam engines became more efficient, coal use went up, not down. The same happened with spreadsheets and accountants: spreadsheets didn't kill the profession, they let accountants do far more analysis. AI will put software into every corner of every organization, and all of it has to be designed, connected, secured, and kept running. The pie is growing.

2. Generating code was never the hard part.
Ask any working engineer how much of their day is typing code. Most of the job is figuring out what the problem really is, making trade-offs, fixing things that break in production, and making systems work together. AI speeds up the typing. It doesn't remove the thinking, and it can't take responsibility when something goes wrong.

3. Someone has to be able to tell when the AI is wrong.
AI produces code that looks correct and often isn't. It can be subtly insecure, slow, or wrong at the edges. The more code AI writes, the more valuable it becomes to have people who can read it critically. You can't review what you don't understand. That's the core of an engineering education.

4. You'll be the one directing ten AI assistants, not competing with them.
The engineer of the near future works like a lead with a team of very fast junior helpers. That's a more powerful position than today's developer, not a weaker one. One person with real engineering skills plus AI can build what used to take a whole team. That's a great time to be skilled.

5. Security, reliability, and regulation aren't optional.
Banks, hospitals, power grids, and cars run on software. In Europe, rules like GDPR, NIS2, the Cyber Resilience Act, and the AI Act put legal responsibility on the people who build systems. Companies won't hand that responsibility to a chatbot. They need engineers who can stand behind the work.

6. The skills transfer anywhere.
Engineering teaches you to break big problems into small ones, think in systems, and test assumptions. Those skills are useful whether you end up building AI, working in a hospital, starting a company, or running a team. Even if the job title changes, the way of thinking keeps its value.

7. Someone builds the AI.
Every AI tool students use was built, trained, deployed, and maintained by software engineers. The field producing the disruption is the last one to disappear.

A tip for delivery: students have heard both the hype and the doom, so don't oversell. Admit the honest part up front: routine coding jobs will shrink, and the entry-level market is tougher than it was five years ago. Then argue that this is exactly why a deep education matters more now. Shallow "I learned to code in 12 weeks" skills are what AI replaces; real engineering understanding is what it amplifies. That honesty makes everything else more believable.
